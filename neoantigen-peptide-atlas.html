Does this delay standard care? Usually no. Designing and GMP-synthesising a personalized vaccine takes roughly 6–12 weeks. Standard chemoradiation (the Stupp protocol) starts ~4–6 weeks post-op and runs ~6 weeks, then a break, then adjuvant temozolomide. Almost every programme here vaccinates during the adjuvant window, after chemoradiation (NeoVax: first dose within 12 weeks of surgery, after radiotherapy; ZSNeo-DC: after concurrent chemoradiation is complete). So the vaccine is generally additive, not a substitute or a delay — but the tumour tissue needed to build it had to be preserved at surgery. For an already-resected patient the first question is what was banked (see “How it is made” below).

How a personalized neoantigen peptide vaccine is made

The core idea: a glioblastoma carries somatic mutations found in no normal cell. Some mutated protein fragments bind the patient’s HLA molecules and can be seen by T cells — these are neoantigens. Sequence the tumour, predict which mutated fragments will be presented, synthesise those peptides, and inject them with an immune adjuvant to raise a tumour-specific T-cell response.

1 — Tissue
Tumour + blood
Fresh-frozen tumour (or FFPE with high tumour content) from the resection, plus a blood sample for the patient’s normal genome and HLA type.
2 — Sequence
Exome / RNA
Whole-exome (± RNA) sequencing of tumour vs normal to call the tumour’s somatic mutations and check they are expressed.
3 — Predict
Neoepitopes
Algorithms rank mutated peptides by predicted HLA class I/II binding. A shortlist (often 10–20, up to ~40 for DNA constructs) is chosen.
4 — Manufacture
GMP peptides
Synthetic long peptides made and released under GMP — the “printing” step — then formulated with an adjuvant (poly-ICLC, Montanide, or similar). ~6–12 weeks total.
5 — Vaccinate
Prime + boost
A priming series then periodic boosts, usually in the post-chemoradiation adjuvant window, frequently paired with a checkpoint inhibitor.
  • Delivery format varies. The same neoantigen shortlist can be given as free synthetic peptides, as an mRNA or DNA construct encoding them, or pulsed onto the patient’s dendritic cells. This atlas covers all four, tagged by format; classic “peptide vaccines” are the free-peptide version.
  • Personalized peptide vaccination (PPV) is a related but different Japanese method: a fixed pool of ~30 mostly shared tumour-antigen peptides, from which 2–4 are chosen per patient by HLA-A type and pre-existing antibody reactivity. It is not built from the patient’s own mutations.
  • Shared-antigen peptide vaccines (survivin/SurVaxM, EGFRvIII/rindopepimut, telomerase) target one fixed antigen for everyone. Listed in an appendix for comparison, not as personalized entries.

How RTK II / IDH-wildtype changes the picture

  • Glioblastoma is usually a low-mutation tumour — this one is better than average. Typically ~1–2 mutations/Mb; this patient’s 11 Sep 2026 NGS panel reports TMB 9.4 mut/Mb and MSS (MSI 3.39 — not a mismatch-repair-deficiency hypermutator; the elevated count is presumably intrinsic to this tumour or panel calibration, not MMR loss). That is a meaningfully larger neoepitope pool than the GBM baseline — a favourable sign for personalized-vaccine manufacturability — but still below the ≥10 mut/Mb bar some programmes use (e.g. Tamavaq in Section 1, and the tissue-agnostic pembrolizumab TMB-H threshold). Some patients with true hypermutation (constitutional mismatch-repair deficiency, a POLE mutation, or later temozolomide-induced hypermutation at recurrence) get far more targets than this; this patient is not in that group. Being treatment-naïve now means no TMZ-driven hypermutation boost yet — a cleaner baseline, not a ceiling.
  • RTK II is the EGFR family. EGFR amplification and often EGFRvIII — and EGFRvIII is a genuine shared tumour-specific antigen. But the EGFRvIII peptide vaccine rindopepimut failed a phase 3 (ACT IV): the tumour recurred EGFRvIII-negative. Worth testing EGFRvIII status; EGFRvIII CAR-T / bispecifics are in the main dashboard, not here.
  • Steroids blunt the response. Across neoantigen-vaccine trials in GBM (NeoVax and others), the patients on dexamethasone were consistently the poor immune responders. Minimising steroids around vaccination matters.
  • Chemoradiation causes lymphopenia. Radiation + temozolomide drop CD4 counts; timing vaccination to lymphocyte recovery is part of most designs.
  • Checkpoint monotherapy has failed in newly-diagnosed GBM (CheckMate-498/-548). Vaccines are usually combined with a checkpoint inhibitor anyway, to counter the immunosuppressive microenvironment — the combination is unproven for survival but mechanistically motivated. This tumour’s confirmed PTEN loss (77%) is the expected chromosome-10 partner to RTK II’s chromosome-7 gain, and PTEN loss is separately linked to a more T-cell-excluded, PD-L1-high microenvironment in glioma — a specific reason a vaccine-plus-checkpoint combination may face extra headwind in this tumour, not just GBM in general.
  • Methylation subclass “RTK II” is almost never an eligibility criterion. Matching keys on IDH-wildtype status, MGMT promoter methylation, EGFR/EGFRvIII, HLA type, mutational burden, and whether usable tumour tissue was banked.

Included because they were asked for — not as a recommendation.

Legal-to-sell and proven-to-work are different questions. Germany’s named-patient / “individual healing attempt” rules and Japan’s regenerative-medicine safety framework permit these treatments; they do not establish efficacy. None of the providers below has published randomised outcome data for glioblastoma. Independent reviewers have specifically criticised delivering personalized vaccines to GBM patients outside trials (selection bias, no controls, cost, conflicts of interest — see the Neuro-Oncology 2025 commentary in Sources). Discuss any of this with the treating team before travel that could conflict with standard-of-care timing.

Glossary

Neoantigen
A protein fragment from a tumour’s own somatic mutation, absent from normal cells — the target of a personalized vaccine.
Neoepitope
The specific short piece of a neoantigen predicted to bind the patient’s HLA and be seen by a T cell.
TMB (tumour mutational burden)
Mutations per megabase. GBM is usually low (~1–2/Mb), which limits how many neoantigens are available.
HLA type
The individual set of molecules that present peptides to T cells. Determines which neoepitopes can work and is required for PPV.
Synthetic long peptide (SLP)
A ~20–30-amino-acid synthetic peptide spanning a neoepitope; the usual building block of a peptide vaccine.
Poly-ICLC / Montanide
Immune adjuvants mixed with the peptides to provoke a stronger, more durable T-cell response.
Personalized peptide vaccination (PPV)
Japanese method: 2–4 peptides chosen per patient from a fixed pool of ~30 shared tumour antigens, by HLA-A type and pre-existing IgG. Not neoantigen-based.
NeoVax
The Dana-Farber personalized neoantigen synthetic-long-peptide vaccine (up to ~20 peptides + poly-ICLC); the reference platform.
APVAC (GAPVAC-101)
A two-part personalized vaccine (APVAC1 = pre-manufactured warehouse peptides; APVAC2 = truly patient-specific) tested by the German glioma group.
Named-patient / individueller Heilversuch
German legal route to give an unlicensed, individually-made treatment to one patient at their own cost — a safety/consent framework, not an efficacy approval.
Regenerative Medicine Act (Japan)
Requires clinics offering cell/immunotherapy to notify and meet safety standards; reviews safety, not whether the treatment works.
EGFRvIII
A tumour-specific EGFR rearrangement common in RTK II GBM. A shared neoantigen — but the rindopepimut vaccine against it failed a phase 3 by antigen escape.
IDH-wildtype
No IDH1/IDH2 mutation; defines the most aggressive adult GBM group. Given here — and it makes the IDH1-R132H peptide vaccine (NOA-16) inapplicable.
